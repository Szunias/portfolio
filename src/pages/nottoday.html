Eleven weeks, one audio person

In 11 weeks I built ~70 audio events and a mix that survives four karts at once: fully procedural MetaSounds engines, a three-tier priority system under a 32-source limit, and character voices recorded by the actual dev team doing their best angry grannies.

Overview

Grannies on go-karts

Four grandmas race tiny mobility karts across bookshelves, kitchen counters and living-room furniture, fighting for the family inheritance. It is a chaotic 4-player side-scrolling party game, and I was the only audio person on the 5-person team.

The audio problem was density. Four players means four engines, four sets of power-ups, collisions and screaming grannies in the same moment. I handled the whole pipeline: editing roughly 450 source files in Reaper, building the MetaSounds systems, wiring them into UE5 through Blueprints, and mixing against a 32-source budget.

It was also my first shared production schedule. Audio decisions had to survive feedback from animators, developers and artists with different priorities. I learned to defend the mix when it mattered, compromise when the game needed it, and ship because four other people were waiting on my work.

What the couch actually hears

Four karts pile into the same corner. A rolling pin lands, a shield pops, two grannies yell at once. Underneath all of it, your own engine keeps its place in the mix, so you always know when to brake, boost or brace for the hit.

Mix hierarchy

How four karts stay readable at once

There is no headphone isolation on a shared couch. Every sound competes with every other sound, and the mix has to decide who wins.

A four-kart pileup at the first corner, two power-ups going off mid-air.

Without structure, that moment is noise. I built a Sound Class hierarchy with three priority tiers: your own kart sits on top, gameplay-critical cues like impacts and pickups come second, and rival karts fill in underneath, attenuated and ducked.

Each tier runs through its own concurrency limits, so low-priority events give way instead of playing at full weight. The engine you are steering never disappears, even when all 32 sources are busy.

Playtest findings

What broke with four players on one couch

Every problem below showed up with four real players on one couch. None of them were audible with a single kart on screen.

The mix

"I can't hear my own kart."

First full playtest: engines, power-ups, collisions and VO overlapped until players lost track of their own kart. I had never mixed anything with this many simultaneous sources before, and my first attempt gave every kart equal priority.

The second pass put your kart at full weight and everyone else at half, which was better but still muddy. The version that shipped uses three priority tiers with concurrency limits per tier, so the local kart, pickups and collision cues stay audible through 4-player pileups.

The engine

A procedural engine can easily sound like a vacuum cleaner.

This was my first MetaSounds project. RPM, throttle and load drive the engine sound in real time with no baked loops, but early versions jumped between frequencies like a broken synthesizer whenever the throttle changed suddenly.

The fix was pitch smoothing: interpolation that ramps the pitch up and down instead of snapping. Acceleration, braking and sliding each got their own movement and pitch behaviour, and the engine stayed readable during races without a single extra looped asset.

The voices

The grannies needed personality we had no budget for.

In the last week of the project I set up a mic and recorded every team member doing their best angry granny: reactions to getting hit, picking up power-ups and overtaking someone. Each character colour is a different person from the team.

The lines run through randomized Sound Cues with cooldown timers so nobody repeats themselves mid-race. The team voices became part of the character identity, and the recording session became the thing playtesters mentioned first.

Production

Under the hood in MetaSounds and UE5

Everything below runs inside Unreal: no middleware, no baked engine loops, and a mix budget the game could actually afford.

I was learning MetaSounds while shipping with it, so every graph had to stay simple enough to fix under deadline.

The engine graph maps RPM, throttle and load onto layered oscillators and filtered noise. The pickup and power-up graphs share a common structure, so adding a new power-up meant new parameters, not a new system.

Attenuation presets and the Sound Class hierarchy kept mix decisions in one place. When a playtest showed a balance problem, I changed one asset instead of hunting through the level, which is what made weekly iteration possible next to four other disciplines.

Carried into Shadow Frames
