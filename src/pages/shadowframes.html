The problem

If the sound failed, the game stopped making sense.

An elderly man with dementia returns to a black-and-white pub where fragments of memory appear as moving glasses, turning chairs and wandering shadows. The player has to find each anomaly, photograph it and pin the image to an investigation board.

Nothing on screen points to the next objective. As the only audio designer, I had to make every critical cue easy to locate without making the horror predictable. I owned that path from source editing in Reaper to Wwise, UE5 integration, mix and weekly playtests.

Gameplay · spatial mix

The core system

One anomaly. Three jobs for audio.

A single cue could not tell the player both that something happened and exactly where it was. Splitting the information into stages kept the player oriented without giving the answer away.

01

Alert

Something changed.

A short stinger cuts through the ambience when an anomaly spawns. It asks the player to stop and listen, but does not reveal the object.

02

Direction

It came from there.

A spatial one-shot identifies the room: wood scraping, a glass moving behind the bar, or a whisper arriving from the corridor.

03

Presence

This is the object.

A unique loop grows clearer on approach and stops after the photograph. The player gets confirmation without a marker or objective prompt.

No marker required

The audio moves the player from a global alert to a room, then from the room to one exact object.

Where it nearly broke

Three problems the player would notice immediately.

These were not polish tasks. Each one affected whether the player could trust what they heard.

70→60%enter / leave

Problem

The threat mix stuttered at one boundary.

When the enemy hovered near the original proximity threshold, the dread layer toggled on and off every frame. Instead of rising tension, players heard a broken switch.

Decision & result

I replaced the single threshold with a hysteresis state machine: enter at 70%, leave at 60%. The 10% gap stopped the flicker, while a Wwise RTPC kept the intensity change smooth.

4 surfacesphysics trace → Wwise Switch

Problem

Manual floor tags broke when the level changed.

The pub used wood, tile, concrete and creaky boards. Tagging every surface by hand was slow, and moving geometry meant maintaining the setup again.

Decision & result

A downward physics trace now reads Unreal's Physical Material and maps it to a Wwise Switch. Designers can add or tune a surface in the editor without touching audio code.

3 kHzobservation filter

Problem

Raising the camera needed to feel risky.

The Polaroid helps the player capture an anomaly, but narrows their awareness of the pub. A visual animation alone did not communicate that trade-off.

Decision & result

Observation mode applies a 3 kHz low-pass, pushes breathing forward and adds close handling sounds. A reversed whisper confirms a successful shot, so both vulnerability and success are audible.

Built for production

The team could change the game without rebuilding the audio.

I exposed system settings through DataAssets, kept the Wwise project structured around gameplay states and documented the rules as they changed.

01

Designer-facing controls

DataAssets kept surface, anomaly and enemy settings in the editor. Designers could tune values without waiting for a new C++ change.

02

Weekly in-engine tests

Every week the team played a fresh build. Unclear cues went back to Reaper; timing and state bugs went back to Wwise or Unreal.

03

Rules, not one-off fixes

RTPCs, States and Switches connected gameplay data to the mix, so intensity could respond continuously instead of through scripted volume jumps.

Performance envelope

I decided what could never be culled.

The production target was 32 simultaneous voices with a 175 MB critical preloaded-memory ceiling. Music and long ambience streamed; camera, anomaly and enemy cues stayed preloaded and received higher priority because late playback would break the mechanic.

The handoff

49 pages kept three schools on the same audio rules.

The Sound Bible records anomaly timing, camera feedback, enemy states, mix priorities, spatial rules and the dynamic music plan. It turned audio decisions into something the writer, composer, designers and programmers could inspect instead of guess.
