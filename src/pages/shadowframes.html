Overview

The game and my role

An elderly man with dementia returns to a black-and-white pub where fragments of memory appear as moving glasses, turning chairs and wandering shadows. The player has to find each anomaly, photograph it and pin the image to an investigation board.

Nothing on screen points to the next objective. As the only audio designer, I had to make every critical cue easy to locate without making the horror predictable. I owned that path from source editing in Reaper to Wwise, UE5 integration, mix and weekly playtests.

Gameplay · spatial mix

The core system

How the anomaly cues work

A single cue could not tell the player both that something happened and exactly where it was. Splitting the information into stages kept the player oriented without giving the answer away.

01

Alert

Signals that something changed

A short stinger cuts through the ambience when an anomaly spawns. It asks the player to stop and listen, but does not reveal the object.

02

Direction

Points to the room

A spatial one-shot identifies the room: wood scraping, a glass moving behind the bar, or a whisper arriving from the corridor.

03

Presence

Confirms the exact object

A unique loop grows clearer on approach and stops after the photograph. The player gets confirmation without a marker or objective prompt.

No marker required

The audio moves the player from a global alert to a room, then from the room to one exact object.

Challenges

Three problems and how I fixed them

These were not polish tasks. Each one affected whether the player could trust what they heard.

Buffer zoneinstead of one hard line

Problem

The threat mix stuttered at one boundary.

When the enemy hovered right at the edge of its danger range, the dread layer switched on and off several times a second. Instead of rising tension, players heard a broken switch.

Decision & result

I gave the system a buffer zone: the tension layer turns on when the enemy gets close, but only turns off once it has clearly backed away. The flicker disappeared, and the intensity itself rises and falls smoothly with distance instead of jumping.

4 surfaceswood, tile, concrete, boards

Problem

Manual floor tags broke when the level changed.

The pub used wood, tile, concrete and creaky boards. Tagging every surface by hand was slow, and every time the level changed, the tags had to be redone.

Decision & result

Now the game simply checks what material is under the player's feet and plays the right footstep on its own. Designers can add or change a surface in the editor without touching audio code.

Muffled worldwhen the camera is up

Problem

Raising the camera needed to feel risky.

The Polaroid helps the player capture an anomaly, but narrows their awareness of the pub. A visual animation alone did not communicate that trade-off.

Decision & result

Raising the camera muffles the pub around you, pushes your own breathing forward and adds the close rustle of hands on the camera. A reversed whisper confirms a successful shot, so both the vulnerability and the success are audible.

Production

Working with the team

I put every audio setting where designers could reach it, kept the Wwise project organised around gameplay states and documented the rules as they changed.

01

Designer-facing controls

Surface, anomaly and enemy settings lived directly in the editor. Designers could tune values themselves without waiting for me to change code.

02

Weekly in-engine tests

Every week the team played a fresh build. Unclear cues went back to Reaper; timing and state bugs went back to Wwise or Unreal.

03

Rules, not one-off fixes

The mix reacts to what is happening in the game — enemy distance, camera state, anomaly activity — so intensity changes continuously instead of through scripted volume jumps.

Priorities

I decided which sounds were never allowed to drop out.

Like every game, this one had a hard limit on how much audio could play at once. Music and long ambience could afford to be second-class citizens; the camera, anomaly and enemy cues always came first — if any of those arrived late, the whole find-it-by-ear mechanic fell apart.

Documentation

The Sound Bible

The Sound Bible records anomaly timing, camera feedback, enemy states, mix priorities, spatial rules and the dynamic music plan. It turned audio decisions into something the writer, composer, designers and programmers could inspect instead of guess.
