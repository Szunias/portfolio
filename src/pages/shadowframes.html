The short version

Over four months, I designed distinct cues for 10 types of anomaly, built a system that guides the player with no on-screen markers, and documented the audio rules in a 49-page Sound Bible for the rest of the team.

Overview

The game and my role

An elderly man with dementia returns to a black-and-white pub where fragments of memory appear as moving glasses, turning chairs and wandering shadows. The player has to find each anomaly, photograph it and pin the image to an investigation board.

Nothing on screen points to the next objective. As the only audio designer, I had to make every important cue easy to locate without making the horror predictable. I handled the whole process: editing sounds in Reaper, building them in Wwise, connecting them to UE5, mixing and running weekly playtests.

What the player actually does

A sound tells you that something in the pub has changed. You turn until the cue feels centred, follow it into the right room, then listen for the one object that does not belong. Once you find it, you take a Polaroid and add the recovered memory to the investigation board.

The core system

How a player finds something they cannot see

There is no objective arrow or glowing outline. The sound has to help without turning the search into an obvious checklist.

Imagine that a glass has started moving behind the bar while you are standing in another room.

A short stinger first cuts through the pub ambience. It tells you that something changed, but not what or where. When you stop and turn, the scrape of glass comes from the bar and gives you a direction to follow.

As you get closer, a repeating sound around that one glass becomes easier to place. It disappears after you take the photograph, so the game confirms the find through the same sense that led you there.

What you hear A stinger in the pub → scraping from the bar → a loop around the glass → the camera confirms the shot.

Challenges

What broke in playtests, and what I changed

The useful feedback was rarely “this sound is wrong.” It was usually a moment where the audio made the game harder to understand.

Enemy distance

The tension kept flickering when the enemy stood at the edge of its range.

The first version used one distance line to switch the threat layer on and off. If the enemy hovered around that line, the mix could change several times a second. Players heard a broken switch instead of rising tension.

I replaced that line with a buffer zone. The layer now starts when the enemy comes close, but only stops after it has clearly moved away. The flicker disappeared and the intensity can rise and fall smoothly with distance.

Footsteps

Every level edit could break the floor sounds.

The pub uses wood, tile, concrete and loose boards. Assigning audio tags by hand worked until a designer moved or replaced part of the floor, leaving the footsteps out of date.

I made the game read the physical material directly beneath the player. The correct footstep now follows the level automatically, and designers can change a surface without asking me to update audio code.

The Polaroid

The camera looked risky, but it did not sound risky.

Raising the Polaroid narrows the player's view, yet the unchanged mix made it feel as if they were still fully aware of the room. The animation alone did not sell the vulnerability.

Now the pub becomes muffled, breathing moves forward and the player's hands rustle close to the listener. A reversed whisper confirms a successful shot, so the danger and the reward are both audible.

Production

Making the audio easy for the team to change

The system was only useful if designers could work with it without waiting for the audio person.

I put the surface, anomaly and enemy settings directly in Unreal, so designers could tune the game without asking me to change code.

We tested a fresh build every week. If a cue was hard to understand, I changed the source in Reaper. If its timing or behaviour was wrong, I fixed the Wwise or Unreal logic behind it.

I kept those decisions as reusable rules tied to enemy distance, camera state and anomaly activity. That made the mix respond to gameplay instead of relying on one-off volume changes placed around the level.

What could never go silent

The game could only play a limited number of sounds at once. Music and long ambience were allowed to give way first; camera, anomaly and enemy cues always kept priority. If one of those arrived late, the entire find-it-by-ear mechanic stopped being reliable.

Documentation

The Sound Bible

The Sound Bible records anomaly timing, camera feedback, enemy states, mix priorities, spatial rules and the dynamic music plan. It turned audio decisions into something the writer, composer, designers and programmers could inspect instead of guess.
