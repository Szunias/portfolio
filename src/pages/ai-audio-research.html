AI Audio Research

For my graduation thesis I tested whether a game can generate its sound effects from text prompts while it runs. I built an Unreal Engine 5 client that asks for sounds during play and a Python server that generates them with AudioGen, MMAudio or the ElevenLabs API, then timed every sound from the gameplay event to playback.

Generated by a model on the spot, a footstep arrives more than a second late. Generation works for sounds the game can ask for early, play again or wait for, such as ambience, so my system prepares sounds before the player reaches them, keeps every result and plays a procedural sound when nothing else is ready.

From the forest path into the chapel

Each generated sound in the demo starts as a text prompt that Unreal sends through a C++ WebSocket client to the Python server. The audio comes back as PCM on a network thread, and because Unreal allows a USoundWave to be created only on the game thread, a task-graph dispatch builds it there.

Footsteps are requested by an AnimNotify on the run animation, landings by a physics event. Only the first step on a surface waits for the model; every later one comes from the cache, and Unreal varies each copy with a pitch shift within 2 semitones, a volume change within 3 dB, a low-pass for occlusion and the reverb of its zone.

The forest ambience belongs to a zone marked by a trigger volume, and entering the forest prepares it. An ambience may arrive several seconds late because it is crossfaded in, so it can be generated while the player walks in.

The chapel door is prepared as the player approaches: the door opening and closing sounds are in the log before the player reaches the door. Inside, the chapel has its own dark castle ambience.

The GPU is shared. The local models run on the laptop’s 8 GB card next to Unreal, which already holds 4 to 5 GB of it, so the server loads and unloads them as requests come in; while one loads, the game plays a cached or procedural sound.

A failed generation is one that comes back silent, misses its length by more than 20%, takes over 10 seconds or crashes the decoder. In a stress test of 500 generations per method, 24 (ElevenLabs) to 69 (MMAudio Large) failed and procedural DSP never did. The server retries once with a new seed, then tries another model, then hands over to the procedural sound, following the rule I set in the thesis: a mediocre sound on time is better than a perfect one late.

Same prompt, four methods

For the thesis I rated 60 anonymised samples, 12 per method and loudness-matched to −18 LUFS, from 1 to 5 for how well each would work in a game. I was the only rater. ElevenLabs averaged 4.3, with clear transients, though it sometimes answered a sound-effect prompt with music. MMAudio Large averaged 3.9 and Small 3.5, with good textures and inconsistent lengths. AudioGen averaged 3.6, reliable but plain. Procedural DSP, the only method fast enough on a first request, averaged 2.4: synthetic and “video gamey”, but consistent.
