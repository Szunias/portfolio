Transientica

2025–present · Solo developer · Unity 6 · C# · KNN · FFT · In development

A Unity rhythm game played by beatboxing into a microphone.

I built the game and its audio recognition in C#. Before a song starts, it learns how that player makes a kick, a snare and a hi-hat, then classifies each live hit against the notes in the beatmap.

Ten recordings before the first song

Players record ten examples of each beatbox sound with their own microphone. The game trains on those recordings, then classifies live hits while it judges their timing against a beatmapped song.

From microphone onset to a judged note

All of it runs inside Unity. The build does not need Python or another application open beside the game.

When the microphone registers an onset, the game runs a 512-sample FFT, measures seven features of the sound and compares them with the player’s own recordings. A KNN classifier decides whether the hit was a kick, a snare or a hi-hat. The hit is then matched to the nearest note on Unity’s audio clock and scored. A short calibration estimates the microphone delay, and the player can fine-tune the offset in 1 ms steps.

Why I rebuilt the prototype in Unity

The first version recognised sounds in a separate Python program and sent the result to Unity. I replaced it with a C# system that analyses and classifies each hit inside the game. That turned a classifier demo into a playable loop with song selection, beatmaps, scoring and saved results. Training data stays in readable JSON, so it can be updated between rounds.

The 2025 figures below come from the player-specific SVM prototype. Accuracy means kick, snare and hi-hat classification after training on that player’s recordings, and latency covers the mouth-to-game path through Python, OSC and Unity.
