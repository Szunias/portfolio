# Portfolio Copywriting Guide for Game Audio Designer

Research compiled for Igor Szuniewicz's portfolio site (EN/PL/NL).

---

## 1. Tone Guidelines

### Target Voice: First-Person, Conversational, Specific

Use first person throughout. First person creates warmth, directness, and connection -- third person feels robotic and distant on a personal portfolio. (Tobias van Schneider: "Third person removes the human warmth from a bio, making it feel less personal and more robotic.")

**The voice should feel like:**
- Talking to a colleague over coffee, not presenting at a board meeting
- A craftsperson explaining their work, not a marketer selling a product
- Confident but not boastful -- let the work speak, then explain the thinking behind it

**Calibration:**
- Professional enough for a hiring manager at Ubisoft or CD Projekt RED
- Human enough that a fellow sound designer would nod along
- Specific enough that someone who's never touched a DAW still gets the gist

### Tone Spectrum

```
Too casual  ----[---X--------]----  Too corporate
                  ^
             TARGET ZONE
```

- Lean slightly casual rather than slightly corporate
- Contractions are fine ("I've" not "I have", "didn't" not "did not")
- Short sentences mixed with longer ones (vary rhythm)
- Occasional dry humor is fine; forced jokes are not

## 2. Words and Phrases to AVOID

### Corporate Buzzwords
These words signal "I used ChatGPT" or "I copied a LinkedIn template":

| Avoid | Why |
|-------|-----|
| "Passionate about" | Overused to the point of meaninglessness |
| "Leveraging" | Corporate jargon, say "using" |
| "Innovative" / "Innovation" | Vague self-praise -- show it, don't claim it |
| "Cutting-edge" | Dated buzzword |
| "Synergy" / "Synergistic" | Pure corporate filler |
| "Solutions" (as in "audio solutions") | Sounds like a SaaS company, not a person |
| "Thought leader" | Nobody calls themselves this unironically |
| "Disruptive" | Tech-startup speak, not game audio |
| "Bespoke" | Overused, pretentious in portfolio context |
| "Strategic" (without strategy details) | Vague padding |
| "Spearheaded" | Resume-speak, sounds inflated |
| "World-class" | Unverifiable self-praise |
| "Seamless" / "Seamlessly" | Filler adjective, says nothing specific |
| "Holistic approach" | Corporate therapy-speak |
| "End-to-end" | Acceptable in technical context, but overused |
| "Dynamic" (describing yourself) | Meaningless modifier |

### Vague Modifiers
These add length but not meaning:

- "Various" -- name the specific things
- "Multiple" -- give the number or list them
- "Extensive experience" -- say "7 years" or name the projects
- "Wide range of" -- list the actual range
- "Helping to" / "Helped with" -- what did YOU do specifically?
- "Responsible for" -- too passive, say what you DID
- "Played a key role in" -- vague, what was the role?

### AI-Generated Text Red Flags
Hiring managers are increasingly trained to spot these patterns:

- Perfectly parallel sentence structures (three sentences all starting "I [verb]...")
- Overly even paragraph lengths
- Generic claims without specifics ("I bring a unique perspective")
- Superlatives without evidence ("exceptional", "outstanding", "unparalleled")
- Smooth-but-empty connectors ("Furthermore," "Moreover," "In addition,")
- Listing soft skills without demonstrating them ("creative problem-solver")
- Every sentence the same length and complexity (real humans vary)

## 3. Words and Phrases That WORK

### Strong Action Verbs for Game Audio

| Verb | Context |
|------|---------|
| "Designed" | Sound effects, audio systems |
| "Recorded" | Field recording, Foley, studio sessions |
| "Implemented" | Middleware integration, engine work |
| "Mixed" | Final audio mix, balancing |
| "Composed" | Music, adaptive scores |
| "Built" | Audio systems, toolchains |
| "Scripted" | Audio scripting, Blueprint logic |
| "Layered" | Sound design technique |
| "Tuned" | Parameters, RTPC curves |
| "Prototyped" | Early audio concepts |
| "Collaborated with" | Specific teams/roles |
| "Iterated on" | Design process |
| "Shipped" | Released games (strong industry word) |

### Concrete Language Patterns

**Instead of:** "I have extensive experience in sound design for games."
**Write:** "I've designed sound for 6 shipped titles, from horror ambiences to weapon Foley."

**Instead of:** "Responsible for audio implementation."
**Write:** "Built the Wwise event structure for 200+ sound effects and tuned RTPC curves for vehicle audio."

**Instead of:** "Passionate about creating immersive audio experiences."
**Write:** "I think a good footstep system is worth obsessing over -- surface material, pace, weight, wetness. Players don't notice it when it's right, but they feel it."

**Instead of:** "Skilled in various audio middleware tools."
**Write:** "Daily driver: Wwise. Also fluent in FMOD. Comfortable in Unreal and Unity."

### Sentence Starters That Feel Human

- "The tricky part was..."
- "What I'm most proud of here is..."
- "This project taught me..."
- "I ended up..." (implies problem-solving)
- "The brief was simple: [X]. The execution was not."
- "My role was [specific thing], which meant..."

## 4. About Section -- Structure and Template

### What Hiring Managers Want From an About Page

1. **Who you are** (name, title, location) -- 1 sentence
2. **What you do** (specialty, not a generic "sound designer") -- 1-2 sentences
3. **What makes your work distinctive** (approach, philosophy, focus area) -- 2-3 sentences
4. **Proof** (shipped titles, notable clients, years of experience) -- 1-2 sentences
5. **Human touch** (something that reveals personality) -- 1 sentence

### Template (adapt, don't copy verbatim)

```
[Short intro: name + what you do + where]

[What specifically you focus on within game audio -- not just "sound design"
but what kind, for what games, with what tools]

[Your approach or philosophy in 1-2 sentences -- what do you care about
that other sound designers might not emphasize?]

[Concrete proof: shipped titles, years, notable collaborations]

[Optional: one personal/human detail -- not forced, just natural]
```

### Good Example (fictional, for reference)

> I'm a game audio designer based in Rotterdam, focused on environmental
> sound and adaptive music systems. Most of my work lives in horror and
> atmospheric games, where silence matters as much as sound.
>
> I've shipped audio for [X titles] over [Y years], working in Wwise and
> Unreal Engine. I care about the sounds players don't consciously notice --
> the ones that make a forest feel alive or a hallway feel wrong.
>
> When I'm not layering ambiences, I'm probably recording weird sounds
> in the Dutch countryside.

### What Makes This Work
- First person, conversational
- Specific (not "games" but "horror and atmospheric games")
- Shows taste and philosophy ("silence matters as much as sound")
- Concrete (tools named, not "various middleware")
- Human ending that reveals personality without forcing it

### Anti-Pattern Example (what NOT to write)

> I am a passionate and innovative sound designer with extensive experience
> in creating immersive audio experiences for the gaming industry. I leverage
> cutting-edge tools and technologies to deliver world-class audio solutions
> that enhance player engagement. With a holistic approach to sound design,
> I bring creativity, technical expertise, and a commitment to excellence
> to every project.

### Why This Fails
- Every adjective is vague ("passionate", "innovative", "immersive", "world-class")
- No specifics (what games? what tools? what kind of sound?)
- Could describe literally any sound designer -- or any professional in any field
- Reads like ChatGPT or a LinkedIn template
- "Audio solutions" -- you're a person, not a SaaS product

## 5. Project Description -- Structure and Template

### What Hiring Managers Actually Read

Per game industry sources: recruiters scan for 5-10 seconds. Hiring managers read more deeply but still want clarity fast. Structure for both audiences.

### Template

```
[Project Name] -- [Genre/Type] -- [Year]
[One-line hook: what is this project in plain language?]

My role: [Specific title/responsibility]
Tools: [Specific tools used]

[2-3 sentences: What did you do? Be concrete.]

[1-2 sentences: What was the interesting challenge? How did you solve it?]

[Optional: What did you learn or what are you proud of?]
```

### Good Example (fictional)

> **Echoes of the Deep** -- Underwater Horror -- 2024
> A narrative horror game set in an abandoned research station on the ocean floor.
>
> **My role:** Lead Sound Designer (team of 3)
> **Tools:** Wwise, Unreal Engine 5, Reaper
>
> I designed the full environmental soundscape -- hull creaks, water pressure
> ambience, distant sonar pings -- and built a dynamic system in Wwise where
> sound density increases as the player descends deeper.
>
> The hardest part was making "underwater silence" feel oppressive without
> boring the player. I used low-frequency drones layered with randomized
> micro-events (drips, distant groans) so the quiet always felt alive.

### Why This Works
- Role and tools are scannable (recruiters can grab this in 3 seconds)
- Specific about WHAT was designed (not "audio assets")
- Explains the creative/technical challenge
- Shows thinking process, not just output
- "Hull creaks, water pressure ambience, distant sonar pings" -- concrete details paint a picture

### Key Principles for Project Descriptions

1. **Be honest about scope.** If it was a game jam, say so. If it was a redesign, say so. Misleading a hiring manager is worse than a small project.
2. **State what YOU did.** Not what the team did. "I designed" not "we created."
3. **Name your tools.** Hiring managers ctrl+F for "Wwise" or "FMOD."
4. **Show your thinking.** The "why" behind decisions matters more than the "what."
5. **Keep it short.** 3-5 sentences of description max. The audio/video does the heavy lifting.

## 6. Game Audio Vocabulary Guide

### Middleware & Tools
| Term | What It Means | When to Use It |
|------|---------------|----------------|
| Wwise (Audiokinetic) | Audio middleware for game integration | Name it when you used it; specify features (SoundBanks, RTPC, Events) |
| FMOD | Alternative audio middleware | Same as above; note if you used FMOD Studio vs FMOD Core |
| Unreal Engine (MetaSounds, Blueprints) | Game engine with built-in audio | Specify UE4 vs UE5; mention MetaSounds if relevant |
| Unity (FMOD/Wwise integration) | Game engine | Note which audio middleware you paired with it |
| Reaper | DAW commonly used in game audio | Good to mention for recording/editing/mixing work |
| Pro Tools / Nuendo | DAWs used in larger studios | Mention if relevant to the project |

### Sound Design Concepts
| Term | What It Means | Use Authentically |
|------|---------------|-------------------|
| Adaptive audio | Sound that changes based on gameplay state | Describe WHAT adapts and HOW (not just "adaptive audio system") |
| Procedural audio | Sound generated by algorithms in real-time | Explain what it replaces and why (e.g., "procedural footsteps instead of samples to handle 12 surface types") |
| Spatial audio | 3D-positioned sound in game world | Mention the tech (Wwise Spatial Audio, Ambisonics, binaural) |
| Diegetic vs non-diegetic | Sound that exists in game world vs. outside it | Use when the distinction matters to your design |
| Attenuation | How sound volume decreases with distance | Use in technical context ("custom attenuation curves for...") |
| RTPC (Real-Time Parameter Control) | Wwise feature linking game values to audio parameters | Use when describing dynamic systems ("RTPC tied to player health") |
| Events (Wwise/FMOD) | Triggers that play/stop/modify audio | Use when describing implementation work |
| SoundBanks | Packaged audio data in Wwise | Use in technical/optimization context |
| Foley | Recorded human-made sound effects | Use for character movement, object interaction sounds |
| Field recording | Recording sounds in real environments | Mention when you recorded source material yourself |
| Layering | Combining multiple sounds to create a composite | Describe the layers specifically |
| Stems | Separate audio tracks of a mix | Use in adaptive music context |
| Occlusion / Obstruction | How sound is blocked by game geometry | Use in spatial audio / environmental context |

### Verbs Native to Game Audio
- "Implement" (putting sounds into the game engine)
- "Author" (creating sound events in middleware)
- "Mix" (balancing audio levels across the game)
- "Sweeten" (adding polish/detail to existing sounds)
- "Layer" (combining sounds)
- "Process" (applying effects to recordings)
- "Trigger" (causing a sound to play via game event)
- "Prototype" (creating early audio concepts)

### Terms to Use Carefully
- **"Immersive"** -- so overused it's nearly meaningless. Replace with specific descriptions of what creates the immersion.
- **"Cinematic"** -- fine if the project actually has cinematic sequences. Don't use it as a generic quality descriptor.
- **"AAA"** -- only if you actually worked on a AAA title. Don't inflate.
- **"Interactive audio"** -- technically correct but vague. Specify what's interactive and how.

## 7. Common Mistakes in Portfolio Copy

### Mistake 1: Writing for Everyone, Connecting with No One
Your portfolio copy should target your ideal client/employer. A horror game studio and a mobile casual studio want different things. If your copy tries to please both, it impresses neither.

**Fix:** Know who you're writing for. Igor's site targets game studios and indie developers. Write to that audience.

### Mistake 2: Describing Responsibilities, Not Contributions
"Responsible for sound design" tells a hiring manager nothing. What sounds? What decisions did you make? What problems did you solve?

**Fix:** Replace every "responsible for" with a specific verb + specific outcome.

### Mistake 3: Hiding Behind Tools
"Proficient in Wwise, FMOD, Unreal Engine, Unity, Reaper, Pro Tools, Ableton..." is a shopping list, not a story. Tools are means, not achievements.

**Fix:** Name tools in context: "Built the Wwise event hierarchy for 200+ SFX" > "Proficient in Wwise."

### Mistake 4: Being Vague About Your Role
"Worked on [Game Title]" doesn't tell anyone what you did. On a team of 50, your contribution could be anything.

**Fix:** "Designed all creature vocalizations and implemented them via FMOD events with randomized pitch/volume parameters."

### Mistake 5: Overwriting
Portfolio copy should be lean. Every sentence should earn its place. If a sentence could be deleted without losing information, delete it.

**Fix:** Write your first draft, then cut 30% of the words.

### Mistake 6: Sounding Like Everyone Else
If your About page could belong to any other sound designer, it's not doing its job. Generic copy = forgettable.

**Fix:** Include specific details only YOU can write. Your specific projects, your specific approach, your specific opinions about sound.

### Mistake 7: Neglecting the "Human" Element
A portfolio that's 100% professional credentials with zero personality is boring. People hire people, not resumes.

**Fix:** One personal detail or one genuine opinion goes a long way. "I think reverb tails are underrated" is more memorable than "I bring creativity to every project."

### Mistake 8: Inconsistent Tone Across Languages
The English version sounds casual, the Polish version sounds formal, the Dutch version sounds like Google Translate. Each language version should feel like it was written BY someone who thinks in that language.

**Fix:** See multilingual section below.

## 8. Multilingual Portfolio Writing (EN/PL/NL)

### Core Principle
Each language version should feel like an original, not a translation. A Dutch reader should never think "this was clearly written in English first."

### Language-Specific Notes

**English (EN)**
- Target: international game industry standard
- Tone: informal-professional (contractions OK, casual sentence openers OK)
- Game audio industry uses English natively; technical terms stay in English
- This is the "source" version -- write it first, then adapt (not translate) to PL/NL

**Polish (PL)**
- Polish readers expect slightly more formal tone than English, but a portfolio is still personal
- Technical game audio terms usually stay in English (Wwise, FMOD, middleware, etc.) -- don't force Polish equivalents that nobody uses
- Polish grammar is more flexible with word order; use this for emphasis and natural flow
- Avoid overly literal translations of English idioms
- Polish has natural warmth in first person; lean into it
- Be mindful of Polish compound sentences -- they can run long. Keep them controlled.

**Dutch (NL)**
- Dutch professional writing is typically direct and no-nonsense; this aligns well with good portfolio copy
- Dutch readers appreciate straightforwardness -- don't over-qualify statements
- Like Polish, keep English technical terms in English (they're industry standard)
- Dutch diminutives and informal constructions can add warmth where appropriate
- The Dutch game industry is smaller; local context (Netherlands/Belgium studios) can be a connection point
- NL can have fewer keys than EN/PL (covered by English fallback) but visible text should feel complete

### Translation vs. Adaptation

**Translate:** factual content (project names, tool lists, dates, technical descriptions)
**Adapt:** tone, idioms, cultural references, sentence structure, humor

Example:
- EN: "When I'm not layering ambiences, I'm probably recording weird sounds in the Dutch countryside."
- PL: Should NOT be a literal translation. Adapt to feel natural in Polish. Maybe the humor lands differently; adjust.
- NL: The "Dutch countryside" reference becomes a local touch, not an exotic detail. Lean into it.

### Quality Check for Each Language
Ask a native speaker: "Does this sound like something a real person would write in [language], or does it sound translated?"

If the answer is "translated," rewrite it from scratch in that language using the English meaning as a guide, not as a template.

---

## 9. Writing Process Recommendations

1. **Start with English.** Write the About and project descriptions in English first.
2. **Be specific first, edit later.** First draft should be too long and too detailed. Cut on second pass.
3. **Read it aloud.** If you stumble reading it, a visitor will stumble reading it.
4. **Cut every adjective that doesn't add information.** "I designed detailed, immersive, atmospheric environmental soundscapes" -> "I designed the environmental soundscapes -- forest ambiences, cave reverbs, weather systems."
5. **Ask: "Could someone else have written this?"** If yes, it's too generic.
6. **Adapt to PL and NL.** Don't translate word-for-word. Rewrite in each language using the English meaning as a guide.
7. **Get native speaker review.** Especially for NL, which may have fewer existing keys.
