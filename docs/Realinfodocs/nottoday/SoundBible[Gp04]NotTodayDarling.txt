


INTRODUCTION
Story
Not Today, Darling! is a side-scrolling racing game with a humorous yet scary premise: four grannies on motorized scooters race each other as a creeping "death wall" (a dark cloud from the left side of the screen) approaches. The story is lighthearted and funny, acting as a foundation for the gameplay. The background is made out of common domestic goods such as scrabble letters, radios, TV remotes, rugs, jewelry boxes, vintage sewing machines, and tea tins, which root the race in a standard environment. The contrast between the everyday surroundings and the absurd action adds fun, but there is also real risk - players must use power-ups strategically to hinder opponents and avoid being overwhelmed by the death wall.. The story has a lighthearted, family-friendly tone with an underlying message: outrun death (literally) with humor and speed.
Aesthetics
We employ a combination of realistic and stylized visuals to create a game world that seems both real and unique. The landscape and items are rendered in a polished, semi-realistic style to ground the scenario, while the characters and interactive parts have a rounded, claymation-inspired appearance. This creates a clear visual separation between the background and gameplay areas, highlighting important interaction aspects. The vibrant art style is complemented by similarly lively audio, ensuring that the visuals and sounds work together to create a humorous, joyous atmosphere.
Mechanics
The main gameplay is an outrun race. Players compete head-to-head on the same track, using power-ups to delay or temporarily halt their opponents. A continually expanding death wall implies that being swift isn't enough; you also need to strategically interfere with opponents to live. The last granny standing (or scooting) wins the race. The scoring and final results take into account both direct competition and how skillfully players handle the oncoming death wall. Every mechanic, from speed boosts to collisions, relates to the primary concept of frenzied racing under pressure.

AESTHETIC VISION
The game's combination of cartoonish action and common background informs our audio aesthetic vision. The sound design emphasizes the humorous, exaggerated nature of the characters and power-ups, while also including real-world aural cues to keep the setting relatable. We've outlined the following major features of the audio style:
SFX
Exaggerated, Claymation-Inspired Impacts: The collision and power-up noises are slightly "squishy," over-the-top, to fit the claymation-style characters and animations. Even large wrecks have a lighthearted, whimsical tone rather than realistic violence.
Standout Realistic Foley: Despite the stylized images, numerous everyday object sounds (forks clinking, scrabble tiles shuffling) are based on actual recordings. These authentic foley details make interactions feel familiar and tangible despite the fiction, allowing players to engage with the world.
Fun and Recognizable Power-Up Stingers: Each power-up activates a distinct, short, and punchy audio stinger to increase excitement and absurdity. We create these noises to be immediately distinct, so players know what effect has been unleashed (for example, a humorous "boing!" for a jump boost or a cartoonish zap for a stun assault).
Character and Scooter Personality: The grannies' scooters rattle and sputter like old machinery, while the characters periodically give forth funny shouts, grunts, or one-liners when racing. These vocal and mechanical elements bring the racers to life, with the scooters audibly struggling uphill and the grannies chuckling when they collide, adding to the game's funny charm.
User Interface
Handmade "Crayon & Paper" UI Cues: Each menu and UI sound has a delicate, handcrafted vibe. Instead of harsh digital clicks, we utilize subtle, organic audio cues, such as the sound of a pencil scribbling or a page flip, to accompany button pushes, menu navigations, and screen transitions. This maintains the interface's playful, handcrafted feel.
Soft Tones for Alerts: In-game alerts (such as an indication that a power-up is ready or a warning that the death wall is approaching) employ subtle, welcoming chimes rather than startling alarms. The alarm sound is audible but not harsh, keeping the family-friendly atmosphere and delivering clear feedback without frightening the players.
Layered Feedback: Where appropriate, we layer multiple gentle sounds to enrich the UI feedback. For instance, when an in-game popup or overlay appears, players might hear a quick paper rustle combined with a quiet bell tone. These layered cues give the interface a textured, tactile audio identity that matches the game’s visual style.
Ambience
Comfortable, Realistic Backdrops: Ambient audio captures the ordinary peacefulness of places like a retirement home or a quiet backyard. Consider subtle background noises like distant lawnmowers, birds chirping, or a radio playing gently in another room. This results in a pleasant soundscape that grounds the action in a real (if comic) setting and contrasts with the high-energy racing.
Tiny Touches of Whimsy: We periodically enrich the atmosphere with glimpses of anything fantastic or enjoyable to remind gamers that this world isn't entirely ordinary. For example, a barely heard tune from an ancient music box or a distant humorous jingle can be played on occasion, emphasizing the lighter mood. These elements are uncommon and subtle, but they provide a sense of inventiveness to the background.
Dynamic Tension near the Death Wall: As the black fog of the death wall approaches during gameplay, the ambient sound will gradually transform. A deep, anxious undertone sneaks into the mix, a subtle rumbling or dark hum that cuts through the otherwise pleasant atmosphere. This auditory transition alerts players that danger is impending, raising suspense as the cheerful noises of the setting give way to a sense of dread.
Music
Soundtrack Inspiration: The game’s music takes inspiration from upbeat, quirky soundtracks like Bomberman Land Touch! 2 on the Nintendo DS. We want tunes that are catchy, energetic, and fun, setting a playful tone that fits our racing grannies theme. This inspiration provides a template for bouncy melodies and rhythmic drive that keeps the races feeling lively.
Music Sourcing Approach: To meet our quality bar within a short timeframe, we plan to source music from a licensed library (such as the Universal Production Music catalog or a similar resource). Using pre-made tracks ensures we have polished, fitting music that complements the light-hearted and competitive tone. If time permits and it benefits the game, we are also open to composing a few original tracks in the same spirit as our inspirations, to give the soundtrack a unique personal touch. In either case, all music will be edited as needed to match the game’s tempo changes and intensity.
Interactive Music Adaptation: The music won’t be static background filler – it will respond to gameplay events to enhance the experience. For example, the soundtrack might become more intense when the race situation gets critical or lighten up during easy stretches. By designing the music in layers or segments (and using Unreal’s audio tools), we can dynamically adjust the music based on what’s happening: adding drums when a power-up battle starts, switching to a frantic melody when only one player remains, and so on. This way, the music actively supports the gameplay’s emotional beats.

TECH STACK
Our development and audio implementation leverage modern tools and a simple, effective architecture:
Game Engine & Code: We use Unreal Engine 5.3 as the foundation of the project. The game logic and sound system are primarily implemented in C++ for performance and fine control. The programming team prefers clear, direct function calls for triggering game events (including audio), which influences how we hook up our sounds (see Sound System Implementation below). Blueprint scripts are used sparingly for higher-level event scripting or quick iterations, but the backbone is in C++.
Audio Creation Tools: All audio assets are edited and produced with professional DAWs. We use Reaper and Steinberg Cubase for sound design, mixing, and mastering audio files. These tools enable us to fine-tune effects, assure consistent loudness levels, and create complex sound sequences before to importing them into Unreal.
Unreal Audio Engine (MetaSounds): We take advantage of Unreal’s MetaSounds system, which offers powerful audio synthesis and modulation features. MetaSounds gives us a modular approach to audio within the engine – for instance, we can set up interactive engine noises or adaptive music without needing an external middleware. We have built a variety of MetaSound graphs for different needs (engine rumbles, ambient environmental loops, etc.), each with adjustable parameters. This modular design means we can easily tweak or extend sounds by adjusting MetaSound settings, and even tie those parameters directly to gameplay variables (speed, player health, etc.).
Direct Audio-Game Logic Integration: By combining C++ and MetaSounds, we are tightly tying sound to the game's logic. We have direct control over when and how each sound plays or alters in response to in-game events. This close integration enables low latency (sounds play precisely when prompted) and high dynamism (game factors can alter sound behaviors in real time). In fact, this implies that if a grandmother's scooter accelerates, the pitch and volume of the engine sound can be updated automatically based on the speed variable; if the death wall approaches, we can instantly swap music layers. Our goal is to create an audio system that feels responsive and alive, seamlessly integrated with the games code.
(References: Unreal Engine official documentation for MetaSounds was consulted for best practices, and Visual Studio is our primary IDE for C++ development.)

TECHNICAL EXECUTION
This section outlines how we implement the sound design technically, ensuring consistency, performance, and integration with gameplay. We cover loudness standards for balanced audio, how we handle attenuation (distance-based sound behavior), our dynamic mixing strategies, and the sound system architecture we chose in collaboration with the programming team.
Loudness Standards
To keep audio levels balanced and professional, we adhere to LUFS loudness standards for different types of content. Our target mixing levels are:
Ambient Backgrounds (Stereo Loops): ~ –24 to –28 LUFS
UI Sounds: ~ –18 to –20 LUFS
Music (Stereo): ~ –16 to –18 LUFS
3D Character Voices (Shouts/Voice-Overs): ~ –16 to –18 LUFS
The whole mix will be monitored to ensure that it never reaches -16 LUFS at peak to minimize clipping or listener fatigue. These settings are influenced by standard gaming audio practices and references (such as suggested Wwise loudness levels). Sticking to these ranges ensures that no element (music, effects, dialogue) overpowers the others, and players will not have to constantly adjust the volume. We will constantly monitor our mix in-engine and modify gain on assets and submixes such that, for example, ambiance remains in the background at roughly -26 LUFS, while UI pings cut through at around -19 LUFS, never becoming too loud. Consistent loudness also allows our sidechain compression (described below) to work consistently because we know the relative levels of music and effects.
Attenuation Settings Approach
Attenuation determines how sound volume changes with distance and other spatial factors in the game. We took a methodical approach to setting up attenuation for our 3D in-game sounds:
Prototype Testing: We began by implementing simple footstep sounds for the characters in a prototype scene, using Unreal’s built-in attenuation features. By triggering footstep sounds via animation events and moving the character around, we could test how the sound behaved at various distances. This early test allowed us to fine-tune the falloff curves in a low-stakes setting.
Applying Attenuation Settings: After experimenting, we applied an attenuation profile that felt natural – one with a moderate fall-off radius so sounds are clear when nearby but drop off in volume at a realistic rate as you move away. For example, we set the scooter engine noise to be audible up to a certain range, with a smooth roll-off so that a scooter on the other side of the level isn’t distractingly loud.
Integrating into Main Project: Once we had settings that worked well, we migrated the same attenuation setup into the main project and attached it to the actual scooter engine sounds (replacing the placeholder footsteps). We ensured that each important sound emitter (scooters, power-up effects, collisions) had an appropriate attenuation setting consistent with our prototype findings.
Successful Results: The attenuation settings carried over nicely – in the integrated game environment, the sounds behaved as expected. Scooters sound close and clear when nearby and appropriately faint when farther away, and spatialized effects like crashes give a good sense of direction and distance. Because we established these rules early on, adding new sounds (like a falling object or an explosion effect) is efficient: we either reuse an existing attenuation profile or know exactly how to create one. This consistent approach yields a cohesive auditory space and saves time tweaking each new sound from scratch. 

Dynamic Mixing Approach
Dynamic mixing is how we manage different audio layers (music, SFX, ambience, voices) in real time to prevent clutter and ensure important sounds cut through. Our approach was iterative and experimental during development:
Mixing Setup in Prototype: In the prototype phase, we grouped sounds into a few broad submixes (buses) in Unreal – for example, one submix for all sound effects, one for music, one for UI, etc. We began by adjusting basic volume relationships between these groups to get a rough balance.
Testing Effects: We experimented with applying various audio effects at the submix level to enhance the mix. This included EQ filters (e.g., rolling off some bass from the music when voices play), delays and reverbs (to test environmental effects), and so on. Early on, we tried a global reverb to simulate being in a large hall; this taught us how reverb on a submix could make the sound more cohesive in a specific environment.
Environment-Specific Reverb: Using a sample map area (a large hallway in a prototype level), we implemented a reverb zone to see how it affects the audio. This small-scale test guided our settings for larger environments in the actual game. Essentially, we established that close, indoor spaces would use a tighter reverb with short decay, whereas open areas (or the outdoors) would use a more expansive reverb. This groundwork means when we design final levels, we already have reverb presets to apply for the desired ambiance.
Sidechain Ducking: To prevent the music and ambient sound from ever drowning out critical effects, we set up sidechain compression on the music submix. In our mix design, loud sound effects (like a big collision or a power-up activation) send a sidechain signal that temporarily lowers the music volume a few dB. We only need a subtle dip – just enough to let the SFX punch through clearly. This way, in the heat of a race, the exciting music doesn’t compete with, say, the “BAM!” of a collision. The prototype testing showed this greatly improves clarity, so we kept this approach. (This technique is further detailed under Gameplay Features → Dynamic Mixing.)
Grouping and Hierarchy: We refined how sounds are grouped by importance. For example, we ensured engine noises and power-up sounds were on separate submixes, since both are frequent; this allows independent control (we can duck engine noise slightly when a power-up explodes, for instance). UI sounds have their own channel too, so an important warning can momentarily sit above other layers. Organizing in this way during development made it easier to manage volumes and apply effects to categories of sounds.
Outcome: Through this dynamic mixing strategy, our audio feels balanced and immersive even when a lot is happening. The music and ambience provide atmosphere but yield at the right moments, important cues (like warnings or hits) are always heard, and the overall result is a mix that reacts to gameplay. The process of prototyping these concepts means we go into final implementation with a clear plan for mixing and with many parameters already tuned for a believable and engaging sound experience.

Sound System Implementation
Our sound system is built with a focus on direct control, efficiency, and compatibility with the game's design. We chose a hybrid approach, combining C++ for core systems and Blueprint for some event processing, which was agreed upon by the programming team to suit their architectural preferences. This is how it works.
C++ Core with Blueprint Integration: The core audio functionality (triggering noises, updating audio settings, and managing submixes) is implemented in C++. This improves performance and allows us to tightly connect sound and game state. In addition, we interact with Unreal Blueprints to make it easier to write specific events or quickly connect level-specific triggers. For example, a level designer could utilize a Blueprint to call a C++ function that starts the race music or to trigger a sound when an environmental threat is detected. By merging the two, we get the robustness of C++ with the flexibility of Blueprint. All dynamic sound alterations (changing volume, pitch, etc. during play) are accessible via Blueprint nodes that connect with our C++ systems, allowing for faster iteration.
Direct Function Calls for Triggers: In our system, sounds are usually triggered by direct function calls rather than relying solely on an event dispatcher or overly abstracted system. For example, when a granny hits a boost pad, the game logic immediately calls a function like PlayBoostSound(grannyID) rather than emitting a generic event into the void. This design was intentional and aligns with our programming team’s preferred architecture – they wanted a clear, traceable flow of events (as opposed to a complex event broadcasting system). The benefit is that it’s straightforward to manage and debug; we always know exactly what triggers a sound. This direct-call approach keeps the system effective and manageable, and it avoids the unscalable “drop a Play Sound here and there” trap that can happen if sound calls are scattered arbitrarily in code (SoundBible_GameProjects (2).pdf).
Event-Driven Sound Triggers: While we use direct calls, we also make use of Unreal’s event system where appropriate, in a controlled manner. Key gameplay events lead to sound triggers through a variety of mechanisms: 
Animation Notifications (AnimNotifies): Certain character animations (like a scooter jump or a granny swinging her cane) have embedded notifies that call sound events at the perfect frame. This keeps audio tightly synced with visuals (footsteps align with footfall frames, etc.).
Blueprint or C++ Calls on Events: For gameplay actions not tied to animation (such as picking up a power-up or the death wall reaching a checkpoint), we call our sound functions from the gameplay logic. These can be direct C++ calls or events sent to Blueprint scripts that then play the sounds. In both cases, the trigger is tied to the exact moment of the game event.
Timed Blueprint Events: For continuous or time-based sounds, we sometimes use Blueprint timelines or timers. For example, the scooter engine looping sound might get a Blueprint timeline to gradually increase pitch over time when a speed boost is active, or we might use a timer to play a ticking clock sound during the final countdown.
Collision Overlap Events: Unreal’s physics and collision system is leveraged so that when objects collide or overlap, they can fire events. We use this for things like impact sounds – if a granny’s scooter collides with a wall or another scooter, the collision event directly triggers the appropriate crash sound (with parameters like velocity passed in to adjust volume).
MetaSounds & Dynamic Parameters: We heavily utilize MetaSound components to create dynamic audio that updates in real time. Our C++ code can send parameter updates to MetaSounds while they are playing. For instance, the scooter engine MetaSound has a parameter for RPM (pitch) and throttle (volume). As the game code detects the scooter’s speed or whether it’s on a slippery surface, it updates those parameters continuously, resulting in the sound changing live without needing to start/stop new assets. This approach powers features like speed-dependent engine noise, or a power-up sound that evolves over its duration. It reduces the need for multiple sound files for different speeds or intensities – one adaptive MetaSound can cover a range of behaviors.
Why This Approach? We chose this implementation strategy for several reasons: 
Fast and Straightforward: Directly tying sounds to gameplay code or simple Blueprints means minimal overhead. We don’t have to set up elaborate event dispatchers or middleware pipelines, so adding a new sound is often as easy as calling a function in the right place. This speeds up development and iteration.
Precise Control: Because sounds are triggered from known points (code, anim, etc.), we can guarantee they play exactly when intended. There’s no ambiguity about what triggers what, which keeps the behavior consistent. It also allows precise syncing (we can ensure a voice line plays after a short delay when a specific event happens, etc., all within our control).
Performance: Avoiding unnecessary indirection keeps the overhead low. A direct function call is cheaper than broadcasting to multiple listeners. In a game that may have many sound events (think of rapid collisions and power-ups in a short time), this efficiency helps maintain performance.
Team Consistency: Importantly, this design aligns with our programming team’s architecture philosophy. They aimed for a relatively tightly coupled system in this project (as opposed to a fully event-driven or component-based approach). By integrating audio in the same manner, we ensure the audio code doesn’t conflict with or complicate the overall code structure. This means fewer integration headaches and a more maintainable codebase. In other words, our audio implementation was developed hand-in-hand with programmers so that it fits naturally into the game’s framework.
Overall, the sound system is built to be reliable and easy to manage, allowing us to maintain a large number of audio events without things becoming chaotic. If changes are needed (say we switch to a different approach for triggering some sounds), the clear structure makes it feasible: we know which part of code or which Blueprint controls each sound.

GAMEPLAY FEATURES
This section details specific audio design elements tied to gameplay mechanics and scenarios. Each feature ensures that the sound design not only adds flavor but also provides feedback and immersion for the player. We cover how the scooter sounds react to different surfaces, the exaggerated audio for crashes, the UI sound scheme, the dynamic audio effects during special gameplay situations (like floating or final stretches), and the incorporation of physics into sound generation.
Scooter Engine Sound & Surface Interactions
Since the racetrack in Not Today, Darling! is literally made up of oversized everyday objects, we designed the scooter engine audio to respond dynamically to the surface type for added realism and humor. When a granny’s scooter drives over different materials, players will hear subtle changes that reflect that material’s characteristics:
Surface-Based Sound Variations: Each type of surface in the game has a tailored sound response when the scooter is on it: 
Monopoly Board: A smooth, cardboard-like rumble. You’ll hear a light plastic-y scratch as wheels glide over the printed board, with a hollow tone to reflect the board’s emptiness beneath.
Pie: A wet, squishy sound. As the scooter rolls through the pie, the engine strain increases slightly (as if goo is clogging it) and you get occasional “splat” noises of pie filling. The overall volume might dip momentarily, implying the wheels are slipping in something soft.
Bread: A muffled, cushioned effect. The engine noise becomes softer, as if the bread is absorbing sound, and there’s a gentle “foamy” crunch as crust and crumbs compress under the scooter.
Rolling Pin: A rhythmic rolling thump. The cylindrical shape causes a periodic bump in the audio, almost like a wobble every time the wheels pass over it. It’s accompanied by a wooden clonk if the pin shifts.
Stack of Books: A papery flutter mixed with thuds. The scooter makes the books rustle and pages flap slightly, so on top of the normal engine hum you hear a rustling whenever you change speed or direction on the books.
TV Screen: A hard, smooth slide. Expect a clean glass-like skidding sound with a faint ring to it. If the scooter screeches to a halt on a TV, you hear a brief high-pitched squeak akin to rubber on glass.
Carpet/Rug: A dampened drag. The engine audio gets a bit quieter and duller because the fabric absorbs sound, and there’s a subtle fabric friction noise (like rubbing your hand on a couch) in the background.
These surface-specific sounds make it immediately clear to the player that the ground itself is changing, reinforcing the idea that you’re racing over unconventional materials. It also adds a fun sensory surprise – players will be curious to hear how each new object “sounds” to drive on.
Dynamic Sound Adjustments (via MetaSounds/Blueprints): We use Unreal’s audio tools to modify the scooter engine sound in real time according to both surface and driving conditions: 
Speed-Based Engine Pitch: The base scooter engine loop increases in pitch as the player accelerates and drops when slowing down. This is achieved by feeding the scooter’s speed value into a MetaSound controlling the engine RPM sound. The faster you go, the more high-pitched and intense the engine sounds, signaling speed to the player’s ear.
Traction Sputters: On slippery or troublesome surfaces (like the pie or a loose rug), we introduce a sputtering engine effect – the engine revs inconsistently as if it’s struggling for grip. This might be random slight volume dips or pitch fluctuations, indicating the scooter isn’t running smoothly. It’s a subtle audio cue that the surface is affecting performance.
Environmental Reverb & Filters: We also adjust ambient effects based on surface context. For instance, on the hard TV screen, we add a tiny bit of resonant reverb (to simulate the sound reflecting off the glassy surface), whereas on soft bread or carpet, we apply a gentle low-pass filter on the engine sound to mimic how those surfaces would muffle the noise. These effects are done through surface triggers in Blueprints that tweak the audio output on the fly.
By combining these techniques, driving sounds different in each part of the track, adding variety and feedback without any extra effort required from the player. It’s all automatic and tied to the game’s physics and material system.
Scooter Falling & Impact Sounds
Crashes and collisions are a big part of the fun in a granny scooter race, so we designed the impact sounds to be exaggerated and context-sensitive. Whenever a scooter (or rider) hits something or takes a tumble, the audio responds with over-the-top flair, tailored to what exactly they hit:
Falling onto a Monopoly board: A sharp cardboard “whap” sound, with a bit of a hollow reverb as the board vibrates. It’s loud enough to be comical (imagine dropping a thick book flat on a table), emphasizing the impact.
Crashing into a stack of books: A heavy thud combined with the chaotic flapping of pages and perhaps a couple of books tumbling off. You hear both the weight of the hit (deep thump) and the aftermath (paper rustling).
Plowing into a Pie: A delightfully gross “splorch” – very muffled and wet. The pie impact is almost entirely a squishy sound, with the expected result that it doesn’t echo or ring out. It’s like the crash got absorbed by gooey filling (maybe followed by a cartoonish little drip noise).
Hitting a Rolling Pin: A solid wooden knock followed by a rolling sound. If a scooter smacks a rolling pin, we play a clunk (wood on metal) and then immediately trigger an audio of the pin spinning away (a rolling rumble that gradually fades). This two-part effect sells the interaction.
Skidding into a TV Screen: A bright glassy clink or smash (not enough to imply it shattered, but enough to sound painful). There’s a short, high-frequency ring, like tapping a wine glass, to indicate the hard surface. We might also add a subtle electrical buzz if the collision is big, hinting the old TV was jarred to life for a second.
Tipping over on a rug: A gentle poof and thud. A fall on a rug is the least dramatic – we use a soft thump (body on fabric) with almost no reverberation. It’s like dropping a pillow on the floor; comparatively quiet, underscoring that the rug is forgiving.
All these impacts are deliberately bigger-than-life when appropriate, aligning with the claymation, humorous style. They also inform the player about the environment: hitting metal or wood sounds “harder” than hitting something soft, which in gameplay terms often correlates with how much it slows you down or how the physics react. By hearing the difference, players get immediate feedback on the severity of a collision.
UI Sound Design & Character Selection
The user interface (UI) in Not Today, Darling! is themed to feel playful and somewhat analog (handcrafted). We extended this theming to the audio with unique UI sounds, especially in the character selection menu, to give even the front-end of the game a fun personality:
Character Selection UI Sounds: When players browse the roster of grannies or randomize their choice, we use sounds that evoke browsing through old keepsakes:
Hovering over different grandma portraits → a paper shuffling sound, as if flipping through an old photo album or stack of character bios. This subtle rustle plays each time you change selection, suggesting you’re leafing through profiles.
Confirming a character selection → a rubber stamp “thump”. Choosing your granny stamps her as “approved” for the race. The sound is a quick, bassy stamp noise, giving a feeling of finality (and a little throwback to old paperwork or library cards).
Using the random selection option → a chaotic shuffle sound. It’s like a deck of cards being rapidly mixed – representing the game shuffling all characters. The sound might crescendo and then stop with a flick, indicating the random choice has been made.
General UI Sounds: For all the other menu interactions and in-game UI elements, we maintain a consistent audio style:
Button presses & menu clicks → soft, tactile scribbles or clicks. We avoid harsh clicks; instead, a button press might sound like a gentle tap on paper or a soft click of a pen. Some buttons might have a faint crayon scribble sound to match the drawn UI motif.
Confirmation actions (starting a race, accepting an option) → a warm analog “ding” or small bell chime paired with a subtle click. This two-layer sound (mechanical click + friendly ding) creates a satisfying acknowledgment that the player’s action went through.
Warnings and errors (e.g., “Are you sure?” prompts, or an unavailable action) → a playful buzz or scratch. For example, a warning might play a brief marker scribble or a “wrrr” sound like drawing a line through something, which catches attention but isn’t jarring like a loud buzzer.
Pop-up notifications (like “Power-Up Unlocked!” in a post-game screen) → a quick, playful bounce sound. It’s similar to a popup animation – imagine a cork popping or a spring reverb – to make these notices feel fun and celebratory.
Countdown before a race starts → an escalating tick-tock. We combine a ticking clock sound with an increasing tempo or pitch to build anticipation. As the countdown numbers flash “3…2…1…”, the ticks speed up or grow louder, culminating in a little “ding!” or whistle at “Go!”. This ties the audio directly to the countdown UI element.
Overall, the UI sounds are designed to be distinct yet unobtrusive. They sit in the background of the user’s consciousness – enhancing the tactile feel of navigating menus – without ever becoming annoying or repetitive. By grounding them in familiar, gentle sounds, we keep the interface as welcoming as the rest of the game’s presentation.
Dynamic Mixing & Real-Time Effects
Elimination-Based Music Intensity: As the race progresses and players get eliminated one by one (until only one granny remains victorious), we escalate the music to ratchet up the tension:
With all 4 players racing, the music is at its baseline: energetic and upbeat but not too chaotic – it establishes the mood.
When 1 player is eliminated (3 left racing), we subtly add extra percussion or a faster beat. The track might introduce hand claps, snares, or an uptick in tempo. This signals that the stakes just got higher.
When 2 players are eliminated (only 2 left racing head-to-head), the music intensity jumps again – perhaps the tempo increases further or new instrument layers (like a racing synth line or intense chords) come in. The music might modulate to a higher key to create a sense of urgency. (This is similar to how many kart-racing games, like Mario Kart, increase the music tempo on the final lap to excite players.)
In the final 1v1 showdown, the music hits its peak: the fastest tempo, most layered, and maybe throwing in some wild elements (a bit of distortion on the bass, a rising siren in the background, etc.). It should feel chaotic and thrilling, emphasizing that this is the climax of the race.
If the match had vocals or a strong melody, it might fully kick in only at this last stage to avoid fatigue. In our case, we use instrumentation changes since we likely have instrumental tracks.
This adaptive music system not only heightens excitement but also communicates progress: even without looking at the standings, a player could hear that only two racers remain because the music has intensified. It’s an audio counterpart to the visual elimination cues.
Death Wall Audio Response: The “death wall” is a constant threat in the game, and we ensure it has a menacing audio presence that grows as it nears:
Initially, when the wall (a cloud of black fog) is far to the left, there’s just a distant low-frequency rumble, like a far-away storm or an approaching train. It’s barely noticeable under the music and ambience, but it’s there, creating subconscious tension.
As the wall gets closer to the racers, the rumble grows louder and we introduce a high-pitched ringing or whine that gradually increases. This simulates that pressure-in-your-ears feeling as danger closes in, and it cuts through the mix to alarm players.
The background music is affected too: as death looms, we apply effects to make the music sound like it’s degrading or being overtaken – a slight filter or muffle on the music, maybe some distortion or warping, as if the presence of the death wall is literally corrupting the world (and its sound). It’s subtle but adds to the atmosphere of dread.
When a player unfortunately gets caught by the death wall, we dramatize it: the music abruptly silences for a brief moment (a shock to the system), and we play a warped, stretched sound – imagine a tape slowing down – representing that player being engulfed. This might be accompanied by a final muffled scream from the granny. Right after, for the survivors, there’s a rush of wind or a whooshing “gasp” sound, as the wall surges forward, and then the music resumes (possibly now in its more intense state since fewer racers remain). Everyone left racing knows something bad just happened from the sound alone.
All these dynamic and responsive audio features ensure that the game’s soundscape is never static. It reacts to what the player does and what’s happening in the game, enhancing both gameplay clarity (through audio cues) and emotional impact (through adaptive music and effects). Essentially, the sound design itself participates in the game’s drama.
Physics-Based Sound Design
Finally, we leverage the game’s physics interactions to generate reactive sounds, making the world feel alive. Since our game involves a lot of objects getting knocked around (it’s a wild race in a cluttered environment!), we set up sounds for the various physics events:
Objects being knocked over: If a racer collides with props (e.g., traffic cones, tin boxes, or any small obstacles in the level), we play appropriate sounds of those objects toppling. For a rolling pin tumbling away, you hear it roll (a wooden rolling sound that gradually softens). For a tin box rattling, you get a brief metallic shake as it lands. For miscellaneous junk like a TV remote bouncing on the floor, you hear a plastic clatter. Each item type has a tailored sound so that a big mess of knocked-over stuff results in a chorus of believable chaos.
Scooter scraping against objects: Sometimes racers might graze along a wall or scrape past furniture. In those cases, we trigger a scraping sound that changes based on speed and the material. A scooter scraping a wooden fence produces a long wooden creak, whereas scraping a metal cabinet gives a high-pitched metal squeal. The faster the player is moving, the higher the pitch and volume of the scrape (using the relative velocity from the physics engine). This gives feedback – a gentle scrape vs. a high-speed grind sound very different.
Books or stacks shifting: We have sections with piled objects (like book piles or stacked board games). If a scooter nudges these, we play sounds of items shifting and settling. For books: a few thuds of books sliding over each other and maybe one dropping. These sounds aren’t one-off; if the stack continues to wobble or collapse, we have a sequence of sounds that can play, following the physics simulation. It adds realism – things don’t silently move.
Rolling objects: Certain objects, once knocked, will roll around (like a rolling pin or maybe an old wheel). We attach looping roll sounds to these that play while they move and fade out when they stop. It’s subtle, but if a rolling pin is chasing a player’s scooter down a slope, you’ll hear it rolling behind you (potentially adding comedic effect and a bit of pressure on the player).
Soft vs. hard landings: We take collision material into account when calculating sound variation. If an object or figure lands on something soft (for example, a grandmother falls into a pile of cushions or that pie), the sound is muffled and mild. In contrast, falling on a hard surface (such as the floor or a table) produces a louder, sharper impact. This was partially discussed in our impact section, but it applies to any physical event; for example, a crate falling on carpet versus tile floor will sound different. This attention to detail guarantees that the physics audio corresponds to the images and expected material behavior.
By tying sounds directly to physics events in the engine (using collision events and velocity data), we make sure the audio naturally follows the chaos players create. If it can move, it makes a noise — that’s the rule. This makes the world auditory-rich: even without seeing a part of the screen, you might hear that someone knocked something over behind you. It’s both informative and entertaining, contributing to the chaotic, cartoonish feel of the game’s world where nearly everything has a voice when jostled.
