

INTRODUCTION
INTRO
Welcome to Cozy Haven, where the days are slow, the tea is tepid, and something dreadful is creeping closer - something inescapable - or is it?
What if this bingo night was your last? What if this apple pie was your final taste of freedom?
But these grandmas won’t go down without a fight. With turbo-charged scooters and an arsenal of knitting needles, they’ll do anything to cheat death.
STORY
In this world, four grandmas who are dear friends are reminiscing in a retirement house. However, at the time they share their dearest memories. A mysterious figure appears to take them to their final voyage but receive a bet as well. When one reaches the shining gate, they’ll walk with a clean state. At this perilous state the friends turn to one another to get to the finish line.
FINALE
In the end, when one reaches the gate, they get to live on as they are but at the cost of losing their friends. As you see at the end credits (if we have time) the grandma looking back at the corpses with x crossing their eyes and having left this mortal realm.
TWIST
However, this can turn sideways as well, if all grandmas get eliminated. All will great death as a friend. And if two or more grandmas were to be reached the door at the same time, none shall pass for it is only one who gets granted life.
EXTRA
There will be no side questing, only a straight path to cross over obstacles, as you traverse these platforms and mix chaos along the road.

DEVELOPMENT STACK
Software & tools
The versions listed serve as the definitive reference throughout the entire project duration. For software - including game engines and modeling applications - any newly released stable version will be considered for an update, finalized only after thorough testing if deemed necessary. In exceptional cases, where a critical bug fix or a feature that could significantly enhance development is introduced, we may forgo extensive testing.
Game engine
ART
Since our game’s art style is a blend of hyper-realistic and stylized graphics, we decided to choose Unreal Engine as our game engine since it offers high fidelity realistic rendering and lighting with minimal effort as opposed to Unity - in which it would require much more tweaking around. Unreal’s default settings are exceptional when it comes to rendering realistic environments (e.g. real time raytracing system). Post processing effects are also way better since for example Depth of Field feels way more realistic in Unreal than in Unity. Furthermore, it offers a vast asset database that’s way more diverse when it comes to materials and assets. Unreal’s built in visual scripting is also great for quick prototyping without much coding, making even easier and accessible. 
AUDIO
Unreal Engine offers a significant advantage over Unity in audio processing, thanks to its real-time DSP effects for sound groups - something Unity lacks due to its missing native submix system. With MetaSounds, Unreal provides a highly flexible and dynamic approach to audio, much like Blueprints but for sound, allowing complete real-time control. Additionally, built-in sidechain compression enables seamless audio balancing, such as dynamically lowering music or ambient sounds when key sound effects play, creating a more immersive and polished experience.
PROGRAMMING
Efficient time management is a crucial factor in development. A well-equipped engine can significantly accelerate the process by providing ready-made solutions, eliminating the need to reinvent the wheel. In this regard, Unreal Engine has been invaluable, particularly during prototyping. Its built-in, robust components - such as Character Movement, Movable, and SpringArm - offered immediate solutions to many of our challenges, often requiring nothing more than parameter adjustments without delving into complex programming.
While implementing logic in C++ introduces greater complexity, it ultimately pays off in terms of performance. As developers, we can seamlessly communicate with artists via Blueprints, allowing them to modify logic when needed. Blueprints also serve as a powerful tool for rapidly testing uncertain ideas without committing to extensive coding. Moreover, Unreal’s sophisticated Enhanced Input system, serialization system and well-structured architecture - Game Mode, Game State, and its intuitive inheritance model - further enhance development productivity.
2.3 3D software
For our 3D pipeline we will be using the Maya-Substance Painter workflow. Substance painter is an industry standard, texturing-focused, software and has a big and accessible material library. As a software it’s a good choice since it offers flexible, non destructive workflow with smart materials, masks and brushes. Further it allows for 3D painting directly on the model, which is a very intuitive and straightforward workflow.
Autodesk Maya as our modelling software is a choice made while keeping in mind features such as the built-in UV unwrapping tools that it offers, or the flexibility when it comes to exporting to game engines. We also have our own scripts that can automate parts of our workflow and speed up the production process.
We will be using ZBrush only for basic and more organic blocking out. Blocking out the character in ZBrush gives it instantly a more organic feel faster and with less effort than if we were doing it in Maya.

TARGET PLATFORM
Our game will be released on PC with a target resolution of 1440p and a desired minimum frame rate of 144 FPS. By default, we opt for the Epic quality setting; however, if we can sustain the minimum FPS, we may switch to the Cinematic setting. In the event of FPS fluctuations, we will consider transitioning to a lower scaling tier or reducing the resolution to 1080p, adjusting the minimum desired frame rate to 120 or 60 FPS accordingly.

Since our game's quality objectives closely align with one of our key references, It Takes Two, we have adopted its recommended system requirements as our own.

TARGET AUDIENCE & CONTEXT
Our game is primarily designed for beginner to advanced competitive players who enjoy playing together in the same space (2-4 players) against one another, with matches lasting between 1 to 5 minutes. The user may have previously played similar competitive racing games (such as Super Mario Kart), making the game mechanics somewhat familiar - such as randomly generated pickups. However, even a player entirely new to the genre can quickly grasp the gameplay through a trial-and-error approach, becoming proficient within 30 seconds to a minute.
We have defined the following categories:
Age: We recommend our game for players aged 12 to 40. If we were to assign an official Pan-European Game Information (PEGI) rating, we would classify it as PEGI 12 - partly due to the target age group and partly because the game may contain mild profanity (BAD LANGUAGE) as well as non-realistic violence against humanoid characters (VIOLENCE).
Gender: Male or female.
Device: PC with Windows-compatible controllers.
Time commitment: Short sessions, 1 to 5 minutes.
Number of players: 2-4 players
Narrative: Since the story takes place in a retirement home, we assume that the player has or had a grandparent or older relative in their family or knows similar individuals within their social circle. As a result, themes surrounding aging and mortality are not unfamiliar, allowing the player to connect with the emotions tied to these experiences.
Game Controls: Apart from basic character movement, the game requires the use of only 2-3 buttons, ensuring accessibility even for less experienced players.
Game Mechanics: The mechanics are not overly complex and do not require extensive prior gaming experience, ensuring that new players can easily jump in without barriers to entry.
Level Design: The realistic level design, featuring familiar objects, will evoke the environment of our grandparents, aiming to stir positive memories in the player. At the same time, the wall-of-death serves as a reminder of mortality, creating a stark contrast between the realms of the living and the departed.
Character Design: The clay-like grandmothers are designed to evoke memories of childhood activities, fostering a sense of nostalgia.
User Interface: A clean, paper-like UI design that evokes nostalgic, cartoon-style memories throughout the gameplay experience.
Emotional Goals: 
Fun & Easy: The game is designed to be lighthearted and entertaining, with colorful visuals, wacky pickups, and accessible controls.
Friendly Competition: Encourages a mix of skill and luck, making it exciting for both casual and experienced players.
Surprise & Unpredictability: Randomly generated items create unexpected moments, keeping players on edge.
Social Connection: Multiplayer mode fosters bonding, rivalry, and shared laughter among friends and family.
Nostalgia & Comfort: Many players will find themselves reminiscing about cherished moments with their grandparents or older relatives.
Sadness & Contemplation: The looming presence of the wall of death serves as a stark reminder of mortality.
Satisfaction & Mastery: After multiple playthroughs, players will experience a sense of skill progression and refinement of strategy.
Comeback Thrills: Rubberbanding mechanics allow players to make exciting last-minute comebacks, creating moments of triumph.
Mischief & Playfulness: Throwing pies, dropping obstacles, and sabotaging opponents add an element of cheeky fun.

PROGRAMMING GUIDELINES
Our coding standard will be primarily based on the Unreal Engine Coding Standard. Additionally, we have drawn supplementary ideas from Laura's (Mostly) Unreal Blog.
While the rules listed here can be found on the aforementioned sources, we consider it essential to document them explicitly. This ensures clarity for developers regarding the key principles we emphasize and are likely to encounter throughout the project's development.
Naming convention
All code and comments should use U.S. English spelling and grammar.
Use PascalCase. There is usually no underscore between words.
Good
LastMouseCoordinates 
Bad
lastMouseCoordinates 

Classes that inherit from UObject are prefixed by U.
class UActorComponent
Classes that inherit from AActor are prefixed by A.
class AActor
Classes that inherit from SWidget are prefixed by S.
class SCompoundWidget
Classes that are abstract interfaces are prefixed by I.
class IAnalyticsProvider
Enums are prefixed by E.
enum class EColorBits
{
    ECB_Red,
    ECB_Green,
    ECB_Blue
};
Boolean variables must be prefixed by b.
bPendingDestruction
Type and variable names are nouns.
Method names are verbs that either describe the method's effect, or the return value of a method without an effect.
Macro names should be fully capitalized with words separated by underscores, and prefixed with UE_
#define UE_AUDIT_SPRITER_IMPORT
All variables should be declared on their own line so that you can provide comment on the meaning of each variable.
All functions that return a bool should ask a true/false question, such as IsVisible() or ShouldClearBuffer().
A procedure (a function with no return value) should use a strong verb followed by an Object. An exception is, if the Object of the method is the Object it is in. In this case, the Object is understood from context. Names to avoid include those beginning with "Handle" and "Process" because the verbs are ambiguous.
Prefix function parameter names with "Out" if:
The function parameters are passed by reference.
The function is expected to write to that value.
If an In or Out parameter is also a boolean, put "b" before the In/Out prefix, such as bOutResult.
Functions that return a value should describe the return value. The name should make clear what value the function returns. This is particularly important for boolean functions.
Class organization
The class implementation follows a structured order: public first, followed by protected, and finally private. After the mandatory GENERATED_BODY macro, each access modifier section begins with functions, followed by class members. Additionally, a blank line precedes each access modifier for clarity.
class MULTIPLEINPUTS_API AMyActor : public AActor
{
    GENERATED_BODY()
    
public: 
    AMyActor();

protected:
    void Foo();

private:
    void Bar();

public:
    float MyFloat;

protected:
    int32 MyInt;

private:
    FString MyString;
};

We organize class members according to the following rules: 
Grouped by visibility modifier, with UPROPERTY-annotated members having higher precedence.
EditAnywhere properties always come first.
EditAnywhere properties with a Category have higher precedence.
The primary category must match the class name.
Categories are separated by | without spaces.
Categories follow PascalCase naming convention.
Categories are listed in ascending alphabetical order.
UPROPERTY members without EditAnywhere have lower precedence.
UCLASS()
class NOTTODAYDARLING_API Foo : public ACharacter
{
    GENERATED_BODY()
    
private:
    UPROPERTY(EditAnywhere, BlueprintReadWrite, Category = "Foo|Audio", meta = (AllowPrivateAccess = "true"))
    TObjectPtr<UAudioComponent> EngineAudioComponent;

    UPROPERTY(EditAnywhere, Category = "Foo|Input")
    TObjectPtr<UInputMappingContext> InputMappingContext;
    
    UPROPERTY(EditAnywhere, Category = "Foo|Mesh")
    TObjectPtr<UStaticMeshComponent> FooMesh;

    UPROPERTY(EditAnywhere)
    TObjectPtr<USceneComponent> SceneComponent;

    UPROPERTY()
    FLinearColor CharacterColor;

    float FooDeltaTime;
};
Portable C++ code
You’re supposed to use int32 for every int that may be serialized, e.g., on UPROPERTYs.
Use of standard libraries
When there is a choice between a standard library feature instead of Epic’s implementation, you should prefer the option that gives superior results. It's also important to remember that consistency is valued.
Avoid mixing UE idioms and standard library idioms in the same API.
Standard containers and strings should be avoided except in interop code.
Comments
For single-line comment, use a space after the //.
Good
// Called every frame
Bad
//Called every frame
Delete automatically generated comments and copyright notices. Remove empty single-line or multi-line comments.
We use Javadoc for documentation generation.
Good
/**
 * Singleton-like access to this module's interface.  This is just for convenience!
 * Beware of calling this during the shutdown phase, though.  Your module might have been unloaded already.
 *
 * @return Returns singleton instance, loading the module on demand if needed
 */
static inline IAITestSuite& Get()
Bad
/// <summary>
/// Singleton-like access to this module's interface. This is just for convenience!
/// Beware of calling this during the shutdown phase, though. Your module might have been unloaded already.
/// </summary>
/// <returns>Returns singleton instance, loading the module on demand if needed.</returns>
static inline IAITestSuite& Get()
Const correctness
Const is documentation as much as it is a compiler directive. All code should strive to be const-correct. This includes the following guidelines:
Use west-const over east-const
Pass function arguments by const pointer or reference if those arguments are not intended to be modified by the function.
Flag methods as const if they do not modify the object.
Use const iteration over containers if the loop isn't intended to modify the container.
void SomeMutatingOperation(FThing& OutResult, const TArray<Int32>& InArray)
{
    // InArray will not be modified here, but OutResult probably will be
}
    
void FThing::SomeNonMutatingOperation() const
{
    // This code will not modify the FThing it is invoked on
}

TArray<FString> StringArray;
for (const FString& : StringArray)
{
    // The body of this loop will not modify StringArray
}
Const is also preferred for by-value function parameters and locals.
void AddSomeThings(const int32 Count);

void AddSomeThings(const int32 Count)
{
    const int32 CountPlusOne = Count + 1;
    // Neither Count nor CountPlusOne can be changed during the body of the function
}
Never use const on a return type. This inhibits move semantics for complex types, and will give compile warnings for built-in types. This rule only applies to the return type itself, not the target type of a pointer or reference being returned.
Good
// returning a reference to a const array
const TArray<FString>& GetSomeArray();
Bad
// returning a const array
const TArray<FString> GetSomeArray();
Good
// returning a pointer to a const array
const TArray<FString>* GetSomeArray();
Bad
// returning a const pointer to a const array
const TArray<FString>* const GetSomeArray();
Static assert
The static_assert keyword is valid for use where you need a compile-time assertion.
Override and final
The override and final keywords are valid for use, and their use is mandatory wherever possible.
Nullptr
You should use nullptr instead of the C-style NULL macro in all cases.
Auto
You shouldn't use auto in C++ code, except for the few exceptions listed below. Always be explicit about the type you're initializing.
C++20's structured binding feature should also not be used, as it is effectively a variadic auto.
Acceptable use of auto:
When you need to bind a lambda to a variable, as lambda types are not expressible in code.
For iterator variables, but only where the iterator's type is verbose and would impair readability.
In template code, where the type of an expression cannot easily be discerned. This is an advanced case.
If you're sure you are using auto in an acceptable way, always remember to correctly use const, &, or * just like you would with the type name. With auto, this will coerce the inferred type to be what you want.
Strongly-typed enums
Enumerated (Enum) classes are a replacement for old-style namespaced enums, both for regular enums and UENUMs.
Good
// New enum
UENUM()
enum class EThing : uint8
{
    Thing1,
    Thing2
}
Bad
// Old enum
UENUM()
namespace EThing
{
    enum Type
    {
        Thing1,
        Thing2
    };
}
Enums exposed to Blueprints must continue to be based on uint8.
Move semantics
All of the main container types - TArray, TMap, TSet, FString - have move constructors and move assignment operators. These are often used automatically when passing or returning these types by value. They can also be explicitly invoked by using MoveTemp, UE's equivalent of std::move.
Returning containers or strings by value can be beneficial for expressivity, without the usual cost of temporary copies.
Default member initializers
Prefer constructor body initialization over default member initializers.
Good
UTeaOptions::UTeaOptions()
{
    MaximumNumberOfCupsPerDay = 10;
    CupWidth = 11.5f;
    TeaType = TEXT("Earl Grey");
    DrinkingStyle = EDrinkingStyle::PinkyExtended;
}
Bad
UCLASS()
class UTeaOptions : public UObject
{
    GENERATED_BODY()

public:
    UPROPERTY()
    int32 MaximumNumberOfCupsPerDay = 10;

    UPROPERTY()
    float CupWidth = 11.5f;

    UPROPERTY()
    FString TeaType = TEXT("Earl Grey");

    UPROPERTY()
    EDrinkingStyle DrinkingStyle = EDrinkingStyle::PinkyExtended;
};
Code formatting
Braces
Braces are always placed on a line by themselves.
Good
int32 GetSize() const
{
    return Size;
}
Bad
int32 GetSize() const { return Size; }
Always include braces in single-statement blocks.
if (bThing)
{
    return;
}
If-else
Each block of execution in an if-else statement should be in braces. This helps prevent editing mistakes. When braces are not used, someone could unwittingly add another line to an if block. The extra line wouldn't be controlled by the if expression, which would be bad. It's also bad when conditionally compiled items cause if/else statements to break. So always use braces.
if (bHaveUnrealLicense)
{
    InsertYourGameHere();
}
else
{
    CallMarkRein();
}
A multi-way if statement should be indented with each else if indented the same amount as the first if.
if (TannicAcid < 10)
{
    UE_LOG(LogCategory, Log, TEXT("Low Acid"));
}
else if (TannicAcid < 100)
{
    UE_LOG(LogCategory, Log, TEXT("Medium Acid"));
}
else
{
    UE_LOG(LogCategory, Log, TEXT("High Acid"));
}
Tabs and indenting
Indent code by execution block.
Use tabs for whitespace at the beginning of a line, not spaces. Set your tab size to 4 characters. Note, spaces are sometimes necessary and allowed for keeping code aligned regardless of the number of spaces in a tab. For example, when you are aligning code that follows non-tab characters.
Switch statements
Except for empty cases (multiple cases having identical code), switch case statements should explicitly label that a case falls through to the next case. Either include a break, or include a "falls through" comment in each case. Other code control-transfer commands (return, continue, and so on) are fine as well.
switch (condition)
{
    case 1:
        ...
        // falls through

    case 2:
        ...
        break;

    case 3:
        ...
        return;

    case 4:
    case 5:
        ...
        break;

    default:
        break;
}
Functions
Always leave one blank line between function declarations and their implementations. However, you may still group functions logically.
Good

void Foo();

void Bar();



void AGrandmaCharacter::Foo()
{
}

void AGrandmaCharacter::Bar()
{
}
Bad
void Foo();
void Bar();

void AGrandmaCharacter::Foo()
{
}
void AGrandmaCharacter::Bar()
{
}
Namespaces
We don’t use custom namespaces.
Physical dependencies
File names should not be prefixed where possible. For example, Scene.cpp instead of UScene.cpp.
All headers should protect against multiple includes with the #pragma once directive.
Forward declarations are preferred to including headers.
Good
class UWoofComponent;

UCLASS()
class ADog : public AActor
{
    GENERATED_BODY()

    UPROPERTY()
    TObjectPtr<UWoofComponent> WoofComponent;
};
Bad
UCLASS()
class ADog : public AActor
{
    GENERATED_BODY()

    UPROPERTY()
    TObjectPtr<class UWoofComponent> WoofComponent;
};
Try to include every header you need directly to make fine-grained inclusion easier: Include What You Use (shortened to "IWYU"). Only include headers where you absolutely have to: don't #include "CoreMinimal.h" everywhere.
Don't rely on a header that is included indirectly by another header you include.
Don't rely on anything being included through another header. Include everything you need.
Sort your #include statements alphabetically. It makes it way less likely that merge conflicts will happen, and way easier to fix them if they do.
Encapsulation
Enforce encapsulation with the protection keywords. Class members should almost always be declared private unless they are part of the public/protected interface to the class. Use your best judgment, but always be aware that a lack of accessors makes it hard to refactor later without breaking plugins and existing projects.
If particular fields are only intended to be usable by derived classes, make them private and provide protected accessors.
Use final if your class is not designed to be derived from.
General style issues
In function declarations or function call sites, do not add a space between the function's name and the parentheses that precede the argument list.
Good
void Foo(int32 Bar);
Bad
void Foo (int32 Bar);
Leave a blank line at the end of the file. All .cpp and .h files should include a blank line, to coordinate with gcc.
Always use the TEXT() macro around string literals. Without the TEXT() macro, code that constructs FStrings from literals will cause an undesirable string conversion process.
Pointers and references should only have one space to the right of the pointer or reference.
Good
FShaderType* Ptr
Bad
FShaderType *Ptr
FShaderType * Ptr
Shadowed variables are not allowed.
Avoid using anonymous literals in function calls.
Good
const FName ObjectName                = TEXT("Soldier");
const float CooldownInSeconds         = 5;
const bool bVulnerableDuringCooldown  = true;
Trigger(ObjectName, CooldownInSeconds, bVulnerableDuringCooldown);
Bad
Trigger(TEXT("Soldier"), 5, true);.
Do not use brace initialization. Use copy initialization instead. Always space both sides of the = symbol.
Good
float MyFloat = 1.0f;
Bad
float MyFloat {1.0f};
float MyFloat = {1.0f};
Pre-increment indexes in for-loops.
API design guidelines
Boolean function parameters should be avoided.
Good
enum class ETeaFlags
{
    None,
    Milk  = 0x01,
    Sugar = 0x02,
    Honey = 0x04,
    Lemon = 0x08
};
ENUM_CLASS_FLAGS(ETeaFlags)
 
FCup* MakeCupOfTea(FTea* Tea, ETeaFlags Flags = ETeaFlags::None);
FCup* Cup = MakeCupOfTea(Tea, ETeaFlags::Milk | ETeaFlags::Honey);
Bad
FCup* MakeCupOfTea(FTea* Tea, bool bAddSugar = false, bool bAddMilk = false, bool bAddHoney = false, bool bAddLemon = false);
FCup* Cup = MakeCupOfTea(Tea, false, true, true);
Avoid overly-long function parameter lists.
Good
struct FTeaPartyParams
{
    const FTeaFlags* TeaPreferences       = nullptr;
    uint32           NumCupsToMake        = 0;
    FKettle*         Kettle               = nullptr;
    ETeaType         TeaType              = ETeaType::EnglishBreakfast;
    float            BrewingTimeInSeconds = 120.0f;
};
TUniquePtr<FCup[]> MakeTeaForParty(const FTeaPartyParams& Params);
Bad
TUniquePtr<FCup[]> MakeTeaForParty(const FTeaFlags* TeaPreferences, uint32 NumCupsToMake, FKettle* Kettle, ETeaType TeaType = ETeaType::EnglishBreakfast, float BrewingTimeInSeconds = 120.0f);
}
Avoid overloading functions by bool and FString.
void Func(const FString& String);
void Func(bool bBool);

Func(TEXT("String")); // Calls the bool overload!
Interface classes should always be abstract. Interface classes are prefixed with "I" and must not have member variables. Interfaces are allowed to contain methods that are not pure-virtual, and can contain methods that are non-virtual or static, as long as they are implemented inline.
Use the virtual and override keywords when declaring an overriding method. When declaring a virtual function in a derived class that overrides a virtual function in the parent class, you must use both the virtual and the override keywords.
class A
{
public:
    virtual void F() {}
};

class B : public A
{
public:
    virtual void F() override;
}
Use TObjectPtr<T> over T* for UObject pointer properties and container classes found in UCLASS and USTRUCT types.
Good
UPROPERTY()
TObjectPtr<ADog> Dog;
Bad
UPROPERTY()
ADog* Dog;
UObjects should be passed around by pointer, not reference.
Good
void AddActorToList(AActor* Obj); 
Bad
void AddActorToList(AActor& Obj);

NAMING CONVENTIONS
Our naming conventions will be based on the Gamemakin UE4 Style Guide and the Unreal Directive’s Asset Naming Conventions.
All structure, assets, and code in our project should look like a single person created it, no matter how many people contributed.
Files
The following rules have been extracted from the Gamemakin UE4 Style Guide, highlighting the most important ones. For any guidelines not covered in this section, the aforementioned Style Guide shall serve as the definitive reference.
Forbidden characters: In any Identifier of any kind, never use the following unless absolutely forced to:
White space of any kind
Backward slashes \
Symbols i.e. #!@$%
Any Unicode character
Allowed characters: Any Identifier should strive to only have the following characters when possible (the RegEx [A-Za-z0-9_]+)
ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
Abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz
1234567890
_ (sparingly)
We follow the basic “Prefix_BaseAssetName_Variant_Suffix” standard for naming our assets inside Unreal. Prefixes allow for quick identification of each asset while suffixes are used only when important information needs to be communicated. 
All assets should have a Base Asset Name. A Base Asset Name represents a logical grouping of related assets. Any asset that is part of this logical group should follow the standard of Prefix_BaseAssetName_Variant_Suffix.
BaseAssetName should be determined by a short and easily recognizable name related to the context of this group of assets. For example, if you had a character named Bob, all of Bob's assets would have the BaseAssetName of Bob.
For unique and specific variations of assets, Variant is either a short and easily recognizable name that represents logical grouping of assets that are a subset of an asset's base name. For example, if Bob had multiple skins these skins should still use Bob as the BaseAssetName but include a recognizable Variant. An 'Evil' skin would be referred to as Bob_Evil and a 'Retro' skin would be referred to as Bob_Retro.
For unique but generic variations of assets, Variant is a two digit number starting at 01. For example, if you have an environment artist generating nondescript rocks, they would be named Rock_01, Rock_02, Rock_03, etc. Except for rare exceptions, you should never require a three digit variant number. If you have more than 100 assets, you should consider organizing them with different base names or using multiple variant names.
Depending on how your asset variants are made, you can chain together variant names. For example, if you are creating flooring assets for our project you should use the base name Flooring with chained variants such as Flooring_Marble_01, Flooring_Maple_01, Flooring_Tile_Squares_01.

Folders
The following rules have been extracted from the Gamemakin UE4 Style Guide and Anchorpoint’s blog, highlighting the most important ones. For any guidelines not covered in this section, the aforementioned Style Guide shall serve as the definitive reference.
Always use PascalCase: PascalCase refers to starting a name with a capital letter and then instead of using spaces, every following word also starts with a capital letter. For example, DesertEagle, RocketPistol, and ASeriesOfWords.
Never use spaces: Spaces can cause various engineering tools and batch processes to fail. Ideally, your project's root also contains no spaces and is located somewhere such as D:\Project instead of C:\Users\My Name\My Documents\Unreal Projects.
Never use Unicode characters and other symbols: If one of your game characters is named Zoë, its folder name should be Zoe. Unicode characters can be worse than spaces for engineering tool and some parts of UE don't support Unicode characters in paths either.
Related to this, if your project has unexplained issues and your computer's user name has a Unicode character (i.e. your name is Zoë), any project located in your My Documents folder will suffer from this issue. Often simply moving your project to something like D:\Project will fix these mysterious issues.
Using other characters outside a-z, A-Z, and 0-9 such as @, -, _, ,, *, and # can also lead to unexpected and hard to track issues on other platforms, source control, and weaker engineering tools.
No empty folders: There simply should not be any empty folders. They clutter the content browser.
Never move folders, only move files.
Do not rename folders: This will create a huge overhead of everything inside that folder. The better approach is:
Create a new folder.
Move all the files in there.
Fix redirectors.
Delete the old folder.
Commit to version control.

Autodesk Maya
Before exporting assets are scaled up x10 to make sure collision is working inside the engine.
To ensure that exported assets from Maya have the correct scaling in Unreal we switch to meters as working units inside Maya.
 
In the export settings we uncheck the automatic checkbox in Units section and instead choose to convert units to centimeters with a scale factor of 100. This ensures that the exported assets from Maya have the correct scale.

Changelist description convention

Whenever we upload content - be it new or modified - we must always provide a detailed description of what was changed, added, or updated in the project. These messages are essential not only for the uploader but also for the entire team, ensuring that, in the long run, we can look back at the project's history and gain a comprehensive understanding of its evolution and development.
Note: the description refers to the part following the type in the first line. The message encompasses the entire content, including the first line and the body starting from the second line.
To ensure clarity and consistency for everyone, we apply a changelist description convention, which consists of the following sections:
#id type: description (mandatory)
body (optional)
General rules:
The first line of the message should be in lowercase.
There should be a single space between #id and type.
The type should be followed by a colon (:).
There should be a single space between the colon (:) and the description.
The body starts from the second line.
#id: The first part of the message should always be a HacknPlan ID.

type: This convention is commonly used in Git commit messages, and we will adopt it as well. To ensure consistency, we will follow the guidelines outlined in the Eagerworks.com blog.


description: This should be clear and easily understandable for the reader. We will use the imperative mood, following the approach suggested by the cbea.ms blog.


It is important to note that this rule may be omitted in the body section.

body: A more detailed and comprehensive version of the description. Here, we are free to articulate the message without adhering to the lowercase or imperative mood rules.
In rare cases, when a modification affects multiple tasks, it is acceptable for the message to include multiple descriptions. In such instances, the message should be structured as follows:
line 1: #id type: description 1 (mandatory)
line 2: body 1 (optional)
line 3: #id type: description 2 (mandatory)
line 4: body 2 (optional)
…
If possible, we should aim to avoid this. Under normal circumstances, a single submission should correspond to a single task.
